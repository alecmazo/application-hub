Marin FC B2013-14 Blue: Passing & Control Report (Veo, Sep 7–Nov 8, 2026)

A shared look at 10 Veo recordings, offered as information for the coaching staff. In the last four matches our possession fell and opponents produced far more — and longer — passing strings than we did. A short two-game trial is one way to test a response.

For the coaching staff

This report is data for the coach's judgment, not a critique of any player or of coaching decisions. The aim is a shared conversation about how we keep the ball, control the middle third, and stay organized when the opponent applies pressure. A possession-based, Barcelona-style identity — keep the ball, build through midfield, and win it back high — is used only as a benchmark for discussion.

What stands out

Pass-string counts come from the six matches that have a Veo match report. Averages that include an 8+ bin treat 8+ as 8, so they are lower bounds.

In the three most difficult recent games, opponents had almost twice our passing strings — and far more long ones

Against MVLA, Newark, and Folsom, opponents produced 182 strings to our 94 (about 1.9×) and 65 strings of 4 or more passes to our 18 (3.6×). That is midfield control in their favor: they could settle, and we were chasing the next ball.

Most of our sequences stop at two or three passes

Across the six reported matches, 85% of our strings were only 2–3 passes long (162 of 190). We reached a string of 4 or more just 28 times; opponents did it 81 times. A possession side needs those longer sequences in the middle third if the ball is going to arrive in the attacking third.

Work rate is not the same as keeping the ball

Against MVLA we made 137 high-intensity runs to their 92 and took more shots (16 to 9), including 7 inside the box. We still had only 37% possession and 29 strings to their 62. We ran more; they kept the ball.

When we cannot play forward, the string gap opens wide

At Folsom our forward-pass completion was 35% (33 of 95) against their 63% (92 of 146). They produced 69 strings to our 36, and 25 strings of 4+ to our 7. Forward passing and midfield control moved together.

A few long strings are not the same as controlling midfield

Newark is the only reported match in which we produced strings of 8+ (two of them), and we even completed more forward passes (53 to 49). They still had 51 strings to our 29, and 16 of 4+ to our 6. The middle third is won by repeating those sequences, which is what a trial with two ball-retaining central midfielders, a runner in behind, and composure in the build-up is meant to test.

Possession and completed passes

The dashed line at 55% is an illustrative coaching benchmark, not a Veo figure. Completed passes cover all 10 matches. Pass-string counts for the six matches with reports are in the next major section.

We had more of the ball in 3 of 10 matches, and we completed more passes than the opponent in 4 of 10. The possession gap and the completed-pass gap both widen from San Juan onward.

Across all 10 matches we completed 1,274 passes and opponents completed 1,624. Our own completed-pass volume stayed fairly steady. The story of the last four games is that opponents began completing many more — and, in the reports we have, stringing more of those passes together.

In the last four matches, opponents completed about 1.7 times as many successful passes as we did (906 to 519, or 1.75×), and our possession fell from about 50% to about 42%. Where we play, and defending under pressure

Pass and possession location shares are Veo Studio figures for the last 10 recordings.

Almost all of our recorded passes (91%) take place in the middle third, but only 22% of our possession is in the attacking third, and only 6% of passes are played there. We spend more of our possession in our own defensive third (34%) than we do in the final third. In plain terms, a lot of the work is happening in midfield without consistently progressing the ball, and a sizable share of our time on the ball is spent under pressure in our own half.

The Newark match report, compared with the previous five matches, fits that picture of defending under pressure: 160 duels (+79), 55 tackles (+20), 14 loose-ball recoveries (+6), 22 interceptions (−7), 15 dribbles (+2), and 2 of 8 shots inside the box (−2). More duels and tackles, with fewer interceptions, looks more like scrambling to recover than winning the ball in a controlled way. That same match is also the one in which we did produce two strings of 8+ — and still finished with 29 strings to their 51.

What the data suggests

When we keep the ball, the games look more like us

In the first six matches we averaged about 50% possession, completed roughly as many passes as the opponent, and scored 15 goals with 7 conceded. In the last four, possession fell to about 42%, opponents completed far more passes than we did, and we scored 2 and conceded 11.

The passing-string problem is about their volume and length, not a collapse in ours

Our completed-pass volume held steady (about 126 per match, then about 130). In the six reported matches, our total strings also held in a narrow band, mostly 28–39. Opponents jumped to 51–69 strings in the later games, and to many more sequences of 4+. That is what a side looks like when it controls midfield and we cannot get on the ball.

The middle third is busy, but it is not yet a platform

With 91% of passes in the middle third and only 6% in the attacking third, midfield is where the game is being played, without enough progression. Winning and keeping the ball in that middle third — turning 2–3 pass strings into 4+ — is the control problem the later matches point to.

When we lose the ball, we spend more time defending deep

A third of our possession is already in the defensive third. The Newark comparison — more duels and tackles, fewer interceptions — suggests we are often reacting under pressure rather than winning the ball back in an organized way.

A short trial is a fair way to test a response

The data does not name a single cause, and lineup data is too incomplete to assign these patterns to individual players. It does support trying, for a couple of games, a shape that stretches the opponent, keeps two ball-retaining midfielders in the middle third, and adds composure in the build-up — then measuring whether possession, completed-pass, and 4+ string gaps close.

A trial for the next two games

This is a proposal for the coach's consideration, not a finished lineup. The request is only that we try a version of this for the next two games and then review the same numbers, including strings of 4 or more versus the opponent. If it does not help, we drop it.

  1. Bruce at striker

    His pace in behind can stretch a set back line. That pinning effect is one way to create the space in midfield that a possession side needs if we are going to turn 2–3 pass sequences into strings of 4+ and play more often into the attacking third.

  2. Gi as the central / attacking midfielder

    The later matches show a control problem in the middle third: plenty of short strings, not enough progression or time on the ball. A ball-retaining attacking midfielder gives us a link between midfield and the runner in behind.

  3. Santi in central midfield

    Alongside Gi, a second ball-retaining central midfielder is aimed at winning and keeping the middle third — the area where 91% of our passes already occur, and where opponents have been producing many more strings of 4+.

  4. Rylen at defensive midfield or in the back line

    With 34% of our possession already in the defensive third, composure in the build-up matters. A calm first pass out of the back or from the 6 is one way to start a string of 4+ instead of giving the opponent the next sequence.

Roles and minutes remain the coach's call.

The data is offered so those decisions can be made with a clear picture of possession, completed passes, passing strings, and where on the field we are spending the game.

How we'll measure the trial

After the two games, we can look at the same Veo report figures and compare them with the last four matches (41.8% possession, opponents completing 1.75× our successful passes, 71 strings of 4+ to our 20, 22% attacking-third possession in the current 10-match sample, and 11 goals conceded). The trial is working if we are closer to the illustrative benchmarks, even if the results are still mixed.

  • Possession: are we closer to 50%, and ideally toward the illustrative 55% mark?
  • Successful passes versus the opponent: do we close the 1.75× gap, and complete at least as many as they do?
  • Strings of 4+ versus the opponent: in the last four matches we had 20 to their 71 (about 5 vs 18 per match). Do we close that gap, and produce at least as many 4+ strings as they do?
  • Attacking-third possession share: does the 22% figure rise, with less of our possession stuck in the defensive third?
  • Goals against: do we concede fewer than the 2.75 per match of the last four?

Data notes

  • These figures come from Veo auto-tracking in Analytics Studio and the match reports, read on November 10, 2026. Auto-tracking can misread events, especially in crowded midfield play.
  • Pass-string counts were read from the bars on Veo's pass-strings chart in each match report. Four matches have no match report — Northern Nevada, Santa Clara, Solar, and LAFC — so string, forward-pass, duel, tackle, recovery, interception, and high-intensity-run figures cover only the other six.
  • Average string length treats every 8+ string as length 8, so averages in matches with an 8+ bar are lower bounds.
  • Pass accuracy often sits around the high 40s or low 50s for both teams — including opponents (overall 48.9% for us and 54.0% for opponents across the 10 reports). Treat the numbers as relative comparisons in the same match, not as absolute completion rates.
  • Nine of the 10 recordings have incomplete lineups, so this report does not use, and should not be read as, reliable per-player conclusions.
  • Individual-match possession in the written reports sometimes differs slightly from Studio's overview chart. The trend is the same; this page uses the report figures for the 6-vs-4 split and notes Studio's 48% overall average.
